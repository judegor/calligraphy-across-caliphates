<div class="info-block">
  <h3>Historical Context</h3>
  <p>
    <strong>Nastaʿlīq</strong> emerged in Iran during the fourteenth century as a fusion of the naskh and taʿlīq scripts. It was perfected by Mir ʿAli Tabrizi and became the dominant style for Persian literary manuscripts. It was later adopted in Ottoman and Mughal contexts but remained closely associated with Persian cultural production.
  </p>
</div>

<div class="info-block">
  <h3>Visual Characteristics</h3>
  <ul>
    <li>Diagonal flow and cascading rhythm of words</li>
    <li>Short verticals and sweeping, rounded horizontals</li>
    <li>Elegant, poetic; less suited to Qurʾanic texts but ideal for lyric poetry</li>
  </ul>
</div>

<blockquote class="academic-quote">
  “Nastaʿlīq was admired for its grace and refinement, a ‘bride of calligraphy’ whose rhythmic elegance mirrored the aesthetic ideals of Persian poetry.”
  <br><span class="quote-author">— Sheila S. Blair, <em>Islamic Calligraphy</em>, p. 517</span>
</blockquote>
